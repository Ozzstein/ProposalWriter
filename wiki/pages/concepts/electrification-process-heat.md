# Electrification of Industrial Process Heat

## Overview
Industrial process heat — the thermal energy required for chemical reactions, phase changes, drying, and calcination in manufacturing — accounts for approximately 20% of global CO2 emissions, making it one of the highest-impact decarbonization targets. Electrification (replacing fossil fuel combustion with electric resistance, induction, or plasma heating) is the most technically mature near-term pathway for industrial heat decarbonization. For processes above 400C, resistance heating (Kanthal elements, SiC Globar), induction heating, and electric arc/plasma systems are commercially proven. Electric kilns achieve 95% thermal efficiency vs 25-60% for gas kilns, providing a 1.6-3.8x efficiency advantage that partially offsets higher electricity costs.

## Key findings
- Industrial process heat represents ~20% of global CO2 emissions; direct electrification with renewable electricity is the most mature near-term decarbonization pathway (source: [[sources/SRC-018]])
- Electrification of high-temperature industrial heat (>400C) is technically proven using resistance, induction, and plasma heating (source: [[sources/SRC-016]])
- Electric kilns achieve 95% thermal efficiency vs 25-60% for gas kilns — a 1.6-3.8x advantage (source: [[sources/SRC-ED-009]])
- GHG reduction from electrification is proportional to grid carbon intensity; Italian grid projected to cross ~180 gCO2/kWh breakeven threshold ~2027-2028 (source: [[sources/SRC-ED-016]])
- Italy NECP 2030 projects ~146 gCO2/kWh (65% renewables), enabling 55-70% GHG reduction for electrified LFP CAM at Brindisi (source: [[sources/SRC-ED-017]])
- No published GHG data specific to electrified LFP calcination — gap filled by analogy from cement/lime calcination electrification data (source: [[sources/SRC-019]])
